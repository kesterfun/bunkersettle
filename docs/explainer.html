The idea in one line

Ship fuel gets paid for only when the delivery and the quality check out.

BunkerSettle is a shared record for the firms involved in refuelling ships in Singapore. The bank's money waits in a locked account, and it is paid out automatically once independent measurements agree. If they don't, a dispute opens by itself and the money stays put.

1. AgreeBuyer and fuel supplier sign the order. The bank locks the money.
2. Deliver & checkA flow meter and the delivery note both report how much fuel went in.
3. Pay90% is paid at once. The last 10% waits for the lab's quality test.
The business problem

Refuelling a ship involves many firms, and each keeps its own records

Bunkering is the industry word for refuelling ships. Singapore is the world's busiest bunkering port. A tanker barge comes alongside a ship at anchor and pumps hundreds of tonnes of fuel across.

The facts of one delivery end up in different places. The meter reading sits with the barge. The delivery note sits with the e-BDN platform. The lab report arrives days later. Payment runs through the bank. Nobody sees one complete picture.

Paymentheld by the bank
  • Quantity arguments. The ship says it got less fuel than it paid for. The supplier disagrees.
  • Quality arguments. Off-spec fuel can damage engines, and the test result comes after the money has gone.
  • Slow, manual payment. Someone has to collect and compare every document before paying.

Why a blockchain

One shared record that no single firm controls

A blockchain here is a record book that every member firm keeps an identical copy of. New entries are added only when they follow the agreed rules, and old entries can't be quietly edited. This is a private network: only registered, known firms take part, and the public can't join.

How the firms agree on the record

A few trusted firms approve each page of the record. Labs don't have to.

The network is designed to run on Hyperledger Besu using a method called QBFT, short for "Quorum Byzantine Fault Tolerance". A small group of member firms run validator computers. Every few seconds one of them proposes the next page (block) of new entries, and the page is final as soon as more than two-thirds of the validators sign it. There's no mining and no energy race, and a final page is never undone.

1Someone submits an entrye.g. the lab files "pass", signed with its own key
2A validator proposes a pageValidators take turns, so no single firm always decides
3Each validator re-checks itEvery validator re-runs the rules and rejects any entry that breaks them
4Over ⅔ sign, and it's finalWith 4 validators the network keeps going even if 1 fails or misbehaves

Validators

Approve pages. Run by a few neutral, well-resourced members, for example the network operator, two banks and an industry body.

Suggested set of 4. Tolerates 1 faulty.

Member copies

Buyers and suppliers can keep a full copy and check every page themselves, without voting.

Optional. Adds independent checking.

Entry senders

Flow meter, e-BDN provider and labs only send signed reports. They are trusted because of their registered role, not because they vote.

No blockchain node. No part in consensus. Just a signing key and a small program at the data source.

So do labs need to take part in consensus? No. Consensus only decides the order of entries and that every entry obeys the rules. A lab's report is accepted because it is signed with the lab's registered key, so validators can't forge or change it. They could only delay it, and with validators taking turns, another one would include it in the next page. Keeping validators to a small trusted group also keeps the network fast. Labs and meter firms are often small companies that shouldn't have to run always-on servers.

The classroom demo runs this design for real: four Besu validators in Docker on one laptop, standing in for four member firms. See the network section of the technical documentation.

What gets stored

Fingerprints of documents, never the documents

Commercial documents stay private with their owners. The shared record keeps only a fingerprint of each one: a short code worked out from the file. Change even one character in the file and the fingerprint no longer matches. The document types follow Singapore Standard SS 709 for digital bunkering documents.

Architecture

Eight contracts, one owner of the order's state

BunkerOrder is the only contract that changes an order's status. The evidence contracts report to it through hooks, and only BunkerOrder and DisputeManager can move money out of escrow. Every contract checks roles against one ParticipantRegistry. SettlementClaim is an ERC-721 token for the supplier's right to be paid. BunkerOrder mints it when an order is funded and asks it who the payee is. Each link is set once at deploy and can't be rewired afterwards.

What each contract does and who may call it

Shared enums, structs, role constants and the custom errors live in contracts/interfaces/BunkerTypes.sol. The one exception is EnforcedPause, which comes from OpenZeppelin's Pausable. Order state machine

Eight statuses, and every way between them

Any call made in the wrong status reverts with InvalidState. Amounts: payable = delivered kg × price ÷ 1000, capped at the funded amount. The first tranche is payable × (1 − retention). Supplier payouts go to the claim holder: the supplier unless the claim was sold.

Settlement also computes an invoice fingerprint (SS 709 DSI) from the order, delivered quantity and amount, and emits it in OrderSettled. Demo parameters: tolerance 50 bps, retention 1,000 bps, retention window 120 s (14 days in production), delivery deadline 7 days.

Signed oracle reports (EIP-712)

Signing separates who vouches for a report from who sends it

The oracle signs typed data off-chain with its registered key. Any account can carry it to a …BySig function, and the contract recovers the signer and checks the signer's role, not the sender's. A meter, e-BDN provider or lab therefore needs only a signing key. It needs no access to a node, no account to send transactions from, and no nonce handling.

  • No replay. Each report is accepted once per order (AlreadySubmitted), so no nonce is needed.
  • Expiry. A signature past its deadline fails with SignatureExpired. The app uses chain time + 15 minutes.
  • Live role check. A signer suspended after signing is still refused at relay time.

Where it fits in the business

Signing helps when the firm that holds the data is an outside provider, not a consortium member. The oracle keeps a signing key. Any connected party can take its report on-chain.

Barge flow meter

The meter system sits on a barge with patchy connectivity and no link to a node. It signs the reading when pumping ends, and the supplier's agent or the port platform relays it once the barge is back in range.

The contract still records the meter oracle as the reporter, not the supplier.

e-BDN provider

One provider serves many deliveries a day. It signs each report, and a platform operator submits them in batches.

No node access, sending account or nonce handling per order.

Independent lab

The lab signs its pass or fail result once. Whoever needs the result on-chain, usually the supplier or the buyer, sends it within the validity window.

Bound to one order and one report, so it can't be reused.

Why not just send a normal transaction?

An oracle could sign an ordinary transaction itself and send it to a member's node. It wouldn't need a node of its own. Signing a report instead of a transaction removes three burdens:

  • Network access. Every outside data provider would need access to a member's node and, on a permissioned network, a place on the account allowlist. With signing, they only hand a signed report to a connected party.
  • Nonce handling. Transactions from one account go through in order, so one stuck transaction blocks every later report. A signed report has no nonce and stands on its own.
  • Dependence on one sender. Anyone can deliver the report, usually the party that wants to be paid. No single party can stall settlement by not sending it.

The signed fields (order, quantity, document fingerprint, deadline) are also readable on their own, so an arbiter, insurer or court can check a report outside the chain.

Gas is not the reason. The Besu network runs at a gas price of zero. On a public chain such as Sepolia, the account that sends the transaction would also pay the gas and the oracle would pay nothing. The contract doesn't refund or charge anyone, so who covers that cost would be a business arrangement outside the code.

What a signature proves, and what it doesn't

It proves

  • The report came from the registered oracle's key.
  • The order, quantity, document fingerprint and expiry were not changed after signing, so a relayer can't edit the number.
  • The oracle holds the right role at the moment the report is relayed.

It does not prove

  • That the reading is true. An oracle could sign a wrong number, and the contract would record it as that oracle's report.
  • That one organisation checked another's data. Cross-checking is done by the contract, which compares the meter figure with the e-BDN figure.

The protection against a wrong number comes from the design around it: separate reporters for quantity and quality, a tolerance check between the two quantities, and the dispute process when they disagree.

Where each oracle gets its data

In the classroom demo the Oracle console stands in for the real systems: you type the quantity and the document text, and the app hashes the text. In a real deployment each oracle is a program run by the data owner, or a trusted platform. It reads its own source system and submits the result with its registered key.

Meter oracle

The barge's certified mass flow meter produces a metering ticket and totals at the end of delivery. A logger or gateway on the barge reads them, signs the report (offline if needed) and queues it for relaying.

e-BDN oracle

The e-BDN platform already holds the electronic delivery note. A service calls the platform's system when the note is issued, takes the quantity and the document fingerprint, and signs and submits it.

Lab oracle

The lab's information system records the test result. A service takes the pass or fail and the report fingerprint, and submits it once the result is approved.

The contract can't check that a source system's number is true. It trusts the registered oracle. That is why quantity comes from two independent sources that are compared, and quality from a separate lab. The blockchain's value is a tamper-proof record of what each party reported, not proof of the physical delivery.

How a signed report travels

1Sign on the bargeThe meter system signs the reading and the ticket fingerprint with its private key. This works offline.
2Carry it to a connected partyWhen the barge has a link, it passes the data and signature to the supplier's agent or the port platform.
3Relay itThe relayer sends the quantity, ticket fingerprint, deadline and signature to the contract.
4The contract checks and recordsIt recovers the signer, checks the role, stores the reading and moves the order on once the e-BDN agrees.

The signature has to be relayed before its deadline. The app uses 15 minutes. A real deployment would allow hours to fit a barge's connectivity. On the Besu demo network the gas price is zero, and validators sign blocks, so there is no mining.

Why tampering fails

The contract never accepts the signer's address as input. It rebuilds the message from the values it was sent and recovers the address from the signature. If anyone changes the quantity, the message changes, and the old signature then recovers to an unrelated address that holds no role, so the report is refused with NotAuthorized. Producing a signature that recovers to the oracle's address for the new number needs the oracle's private key. The signature is not a hash of the key: it is made from the message and the key together, so it works only for that exact message.

Limits to know about

  • Key custody. A stolen oracle key produces valid but false reports. The registry can revoke or suspend the role, after which signatures from that key fail at relay time.
  • Withholding. Whoever holds a signature can decline to send it. The delivery deadline and the dispute process cover that case.
  • Two signatures for one order. The first one relayed wins, so an oracle should sign one report per order.
  • Wrong data at the source. A miscalibrated meter signs a false number validly. That is why the e-BDN is a second, independent source and the lab reports separately.

  • Validator keys are committed for reproducibility. They are dev-only and secure nothing real.

Fault-tolerance demo

  • Stop validator 2, 3 or 4. Its card turns red in the app's Network panel and blocks keep coming.
  • Stop a second. Only 2 of 4 remain, so the panel shows "Halted — needs 3 of 4 validators".
  • Start them again. The chain resumes within about 2 minutes as QBFT's round timer backs off.
  • Never stop validator 1 during the demo: the app reads through it.
Web app

A React app that talks to the chain directly

Vite, React 18 and ethers 6, with no backend. Reads go through a JSON-RPC provider, so the app works before a wallet connects. Writes are signed in MetaMask. Every revert is decoded against all eight ABIs and shown by name.
